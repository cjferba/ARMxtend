ARM.anomaly -- anomalies as rule violations

Rules mined on a period of normal behaviour describe what usually happens. A row that breaks a reliable rule -- its antecedent holds and its consequent does not -- is a candidate anomaly, the more so the more reliable the rule. This is the idea of exception rules, applied to new data with rules that have already been mined, including lagged rules: "the door opened one step ago and the heater has not switched on".

from ARMxtend.ARM import (
    temporal_association_rules, significant_rules, prune_unproductive_rules, detect_anomalies,
)

rules = temporal_association_rules(train, 1, 0.02, min_threshold=0.75, targets=["light", "chime"],
                                   exclude_persistence=True, same_time_antecedent=False)
rules = significant_rules(rules, alpha=0.05)                                    # drop rules that are noise
rules = prune_unproductive_rules(rules[rules["consequents"].map(len) == 1], min_improvement=0.05)

flagged = detect_anomalies(test, rules, min_score=6.0)
flagged.iloc[0]["rules"]      # ['motion (t-1) => light (t)', 'door (t-1) => chime (t)']

A full run is in the example.

Violations and scores

A row violates A => C if all the items of A hold and not all the items of C do. Items with a lag (x@lag2, see ARM.temporal_rules) are read from earlier rows; the first rows, without enough history for a rule, never violate it (to analyse a new period with history, include the previous rows in items and select the period afterwards).

Function Returns
rule_violations(items, rules) boolean table, one row per row of items and one column per rule (with the index of rules)
anomaly_scores(items, rules, weight="surprise", floor=1e-3) score, violations and applicable (rules whose antecedent holds) for each row
detect_anomalies(items, rules, min_score=0.0, top=None, ...) the rows with a score above min_score, highest first, with the violated rules written out

The score of a row is the sum of the weights of the rules it violates:

weight Weight of a violated rule Meaning
surprise (default) -log2(1 - confidence) bits of surprise of a violation if the rule held with probability equal to its confidence: breaking a 99 % rule weighs 6.6 bits, an 80 % rule 2.3
confidence the confidence
count 1 number of rules violated

A rule of confidence 1 would weigh infinity: floor bounds the violation probability from below (default 1e-3, so at most 10 bits).

In the example, a light that fails to switch on after motion is injected 30 times in 2000 steps, while a weak rule (door => chime, 77 %) is violated by chance in about 4 % of the rows. With surprise the AUC is 0.998 and the 30 faults are all among the 39 rows above 6 bits (the other 9 are the 1 % of times the light fails by itself); counting violated rules gives 0.980 and cannot tell the faults apart from the harmless violations of the weak rule.

Getting good rules

The quality of the alarms is the quality of the rules:

  • Mine on normal data. If faults are common in the training period they become part of the "normal" rules.
  • Keep only real rules with significant_rules: a rule that is just noise raises false alarms.
  • Prune with prune_redundant_rules and prune_unproductive_rules: rules that differ in an irrelevant extra condition are violated together and their weights add up. Restricting to rules with a single item as consequent also avoids counting one failure twice.
  • Use same_time_antecedent=False (and exclude_persistence=True) when mining, so that the rules say what should happen after what already happened.
  • Natural violations of a very reliable rule are indistinguishable from faults: with a 99 % rule, 1 in 100 triggers will look like a fault. The score ranks; deciding the threshold is up to the application.

Command line: armxtend anomalies.
